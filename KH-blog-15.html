📌📌 Today's Message: The Woman Who Refused to Let the Ruins Be Forgotten 🌺

Mary Riter Hamilton was born in Teeswater, Ontario, in 1867, and by every rule of her time she was supposed to live a small, quiet life. 🌾 She married at 21, was widowed at 26, and instead of folding herself into the life the world had planned for her, she picked up a brush, opened a china painting school, and then crossed the Atlantic to study art in Berlin and Paris, where one of her paintings hung in the Salon, the French Academy's own exhibition. 🎨 She came home to Canada in 1911 to care for her dying mother, and spent the war years painting the Rockies and the prairies of Western Canada, watching from a distance while a generation of young men sailed away to France. 🇨🇦 When she applied to go to the front as an official war artist, the answer came back no, because women were not sent to war zones. She refused to accept that as the end of it. 🚫

In March 1919, four months after the guns fell silent, Mary sailed for France on a commission from the Amputation Club of British Columbia, the veterans' group that would become the War Amps, to paint the battlefields for their magazine, The Gold Stripe. 📰 She arrived on April 13, 1919, a 51-year-old woman travelling alone into a landscape of mud, craters, and ruined towns, and she set up her easel right there in the wreckage, at the Somme, at Vimy Ridge, at Ypres, Arras, and Cambrai, painting the ground where Canadians had fought and died before anyone cleared it away. 🏚️ She lived in a tin hut and in disused army shelters, camped among the ruins with her dog Old Bob at her side, and worked through three years of cold, damp, and danger while the Chinese labour corps cleared the debris of the Western Front around her. 🐕 She painted more than 320 works in those three years, the largest collection of First World War paintings by any single Canadian artist, and she called the work her gift to Canada. 🎁

Look at what she chose to paint and you will see why her work matters so much on a day like today. 🌺 She did not paint glory or victory. She painted Trenches on the Somme, a landscape torn open and left bleeding. She painted Isolated Grave and Camouflage at Vimy Ridge, one small cross standing in a field that had swallowed thousands. She painted Market Among the Ruins of Ypres, where survivors were already setting up stalls among the rubble and getting on with living. 🏪 France saw what she had done and awarded her Les Palmes Academiques, one of its highest honours, the only Canadian artist ever recognised that way, and her battlefield art took the gold medal at the International Decorative Arts Exhibition in 1925. 🏅 She went blind in one eye from an illness in 1923, she donated more than 220 of her battlefield paintings to the Public Archives of Canada in the mid-1920s so the country would never lose them, and she died in 1954 in a psychiatric hospital in Coquitlam, largely forgotten, buried beside the husband she had lost sixty years earlier. 🕯️

She refused to let the ruins be forgotten, and because she refused, we can still stand in front of a canvas today and see what Vimy looked like before the grass grew back. 🌹 The poppy I wear today is for the ones in uniform, and it is also for the ones like Mary, who went to the wreckage with nothing but a brush and a stubborn heart and came home with the truth in her hands. 📖 If her name is new to you, say it once out loud today, and tell one other person, because that is how the forgotten get remembered, and that is how a woman who was told no ended up painting the largest record of Canada's war that any single artist ever made. Forward is always a choice, and that means YOU too!

🛠️🌿 The Deep Dive: 5 Things Most Pro Landscapers Won't Tell You 🌿🛠️

1. Find the Weeping-Tile Discharge Before the Ground Locks 🕳️
Every foundation drain has a discharge point, and most of them are forgotten until the basement tells you about it in March. Find yours now while the ground still digs: follow the line from the sump or the foundation cleanout to where it daylights, and run a hose through it. If it trickles, it is silted; if it does not drain at all, it is crushed or root-blocked, and a chinook melt with nowhere to go will sit against your footing all winter and push water through the first crack it finds. Mark a daylighted outlet with a tall stake so the snow crew does not pile over it, and clear the grate, because a buried outlet is a flooded spring waiting its turn.

2. Last Honest Concrete Window: Pour by Wednesday 🧱
Concrete needs about 5 degrees and rising to cure properly, and the forecast turns on Thursday, with overnight lows dropping to minus 1 and colder from there. Any small pour still on the list, a post footing, a paver bedding pad, a step repair, has to go in by Wednesday, and it has to be covered and tented overnight so the surface never sees frost before it sets. A pour that freezes in its first 24 hours never reaches its strength, and it will scale and crumble by spring no matter how good the mix was. If you cannot pour by Wednesday, wait for spring, because a delayed pour costs you a season and a frozen pour costs you the whole job.

3. Straw-Mulch Tender Perennials After the Ground Freezes 🌾
Lavender, butterfly bush, fall-planted roses and any perennial sitting one zone past its comfort line all want a blanket, but the timing is everything. Wait until the ground has actually frozen, then tuck loose straw around the crowns, about 15 to 20 centimetres deep, and leave the crown itself breathing. Straw insulates without matting down into a wet slab the way leaves do, and it lets air move so the crown does not rot. Mulch too early and you build a mouse condo with room service, because rodents move into warm dry straw the same week you lay it, so let the freeze come first and let the straw do its job all winter.

4. Salted Slush Goes Street-Side, Never Onto Beds or Lawn 🧂
The first salted slush of the season is the most damaging load your lawn and beds will take all winter, and where you pile it decides whether spring starts with green edges or dead strips. Sodium in meltwater burns turf roots and perennial crowns, and a single pile of street slush dumped on a bed will salt that soil until the spring rains flush it out. Push salted slush to the street side of the drive and walk, never onto the lawn, the beds, or the base of a tree, and keep the clean snow for the beds, where it insulates. It is a ten-second habit that saves a whole spring of patching.

5. Drain the Backflow Preventer After the Blowout 💧
A blowout clears the zones, but the backflow preventer sitting above ground on the side of the house is still full of water, and it is the most expensive freeze casualty in the whole irrigation system. After the blowout, shut the isolation valves, open both test cocks on the device, and let it drain completely, then leave the test cocks open at a 45-degree angle so any last drops have somewhere to go. A cracked brass body costs several hundred dollars and a spring morning of phone calls, and it is a two-minute job in a mild week like this one. If the device is in an unheated garage, bring it inside for the winter, because a single hard night is all it takes.

🇨🇦 Canadian Track'O'The Day 🎶

Summer of '69 - Bryan Adams (clean). Kingston, Ontario's Bryan Adams wrote this one with his partner Jim Vallance, and it landed on Reckless in 1984, the album that turned him from a Canadian rocker into a worldwide name. It is a song about being 18 and believing the summer would never end, and for me it is the sound of a chinook afternoon in November, warm air in the middle of a cold season, the past showing up uninvited and welcome. Play it on the morning drive, windows down while you still can, and let it set the tone before the tools come out. 🚗

🤣 Today's Musical Funny-Bone 🦴

The Elements - Tom Lehrer (clean). Tom Lehrer was a Harvard mathematician who moonlighted as America's sharpest musical satirist, and in 1959 he set the names of all 102 known chemical elements to the tune of Gilbert and Sullivan's Major-General's Song and sang the whole list in about ninety seconds flat. It is the funniest chemistry lesson ever recorded, and it has been making science kids grin for more than sixty years. Play it when the crew needs morale in its purest, nerdiest form: a laugh that also makes you smarter. 😄

🤘 Hair Whippin', Head Bangin', Finger Blazin' Therapy 🔥

Master of Puppets - Metallica (clean). The title track of Metallica's 1986 album is eight and a half minutes of thrash metal at its absolute peak, James Hetfield and Kirk Hammett trading riffs like a prize fight, and Cliff Burton's bass holding the whole thing together on the last record he ever made. The song is about addiction wearing a puppet master's strings, which makes it heavier than the riffs, and it has been called one of the greatest metal songs ever written by just about everybody who writes those lists. Play it loud on the last job of the day and finish the list like you mean it. 🤘

🎤 Tongue Trippin', Bar Spittin, Beat Killin Bars 🔥

N.Y. State of Mind - Nas [E] (explicit language throughout). Nasir Jones was barely twenty and straight out of the Queensbridge projects when he opened Illmatic in 1994 with this verse over DJ Premier's beat, and it is still talked about as one of the coldest opening tracks in hip-hop history. Illmatic went on to be called one of the greatest hip-hop albums ever made, and this track is the reason the first ninety seconds of the record already had people rewinding. For my money this is tailor-made for the 6am version of me, boots half-laced, talking himself into the day. Play it before the first shovel hits the ground. 🥾

🇳🇿 Māori Track / Aotearoa Pick 🌿

Aotearoa - Stan Walker ft. Ria Hall, Troy Kingi and Maisey Rika (clean). Stan Walker is Maori, Ngapuhi, raised in Tauranga, and in 2014 he gathered three more Maori voices, Ria Hall of Ngai Te Rangi, Troy Kingi of Ngapuhi and Te Arawa, and Maisey Rika of Ngati Awa, Tuhoe, Te Arawa and Te Whanau-a-Apanui, to sing this love letter to the land in te reo Maori and English. It is a song about belonging to a place and a place belonging to you, which is the whole reason I keep one foot in Edmonton and one in Aotearoa. Play it on the drive home with the heater on and let the harmonies do the talking. 🌿

🌟 Ron Hairemy Spotlight ✨

Goats'N'Sheep - Ron Hairemy ft. Kohr36 (clean). This one is mine, and it sits in the playlist because it belongs there: my own cut with Kohr36, who has been in my corner since the Craigles days and sang on Can't Be Stopped with me. Putting our track in the spotlight on Remembrance Day feels right, because the day is about the people who stood their ground, and the whole point of making music with your own people is standing together. A spotlight is supposed to show you what somebody loves enough to keep, and what I keep coming back to is this: the work, the words, and the ones who were there for both. That's the goal. That's LAWNgevity. 🌱🔥

Mary Riter Hamilton had a commission and refused to let the ruins be forgotten, and that one refusal is still echoing more than a century later, which tells you what a single stubborn, decent decision is worth. 🌹 Your action hook today is Tip 1: before the ground locks, find your weeping-tile discharge and run a hose through it, because the meltwater you free this week is the basement you do not flood in March.
