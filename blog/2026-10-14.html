🌟 Today's Message: Do Something: Gord Downie's Last Gift

Let me tell you about Gord Downie 🇨🇦🎤, frontman of The Tragically Hip poet laureate of every small-town Canadian summer, a man whose voice basically raised a generation of us 🌅. In May 2016 he got the diagnosis nobody survives: terminal brain cancer, glioblastoma 🧠💔. He was 52 years old, at the peak of his powers, and the clock had just started screaming ⏳. A lot of people would have gone quiet. Gord went LOUDER 🔊.

He took the Hip on one final cross-Canada tour 🎸🇨🇦, sold out from Victoria to Kingston while the cancer took more of him every night, and on August 20, 2016, in his hometown of Kingston, he played the last show to a country holding its breath 📺❤️. Millions watched. And in the middle of his goodbye, with the Prime Minister sitting in the crowd, Gord pointed the biggest spotlight of his life away from himself, at Canada's treatment of Indigenous peoples, and told Justin Trudeau, on live television, that he had to do something about it 🪶🔥. A dying man, using his farewell as a megaphone for people this country had ignored for 150 years. Let that sit in your chest for a second 💓.

What had hold of him was the story of Chanie Wenjack 🕯️, a 12-year-old Ojibwe boy taken to the Cecilia Jeffrey Indian Residential School in Kenora, who ran away in October 1966 trying to walk 600 kilometres home to the family he'd been taken from 🚶‍♂️🌲. Chanie's body was found beside the railway tracks on October 22, 1966, he died of starvation and exposure, a child, alone, trying to get home 💔❄️. Gord first heard the story from his brother Mike, and he simply could not let it go. He turned it into Secret Path by an album, a graphic novel with Jeff Lemire, an animated film, released in October 2016, fifty years after Chanie's death 📖🎶. Every dollar of proceeds went to truth and reconciliation work.

Gord died on October 17, 2017 🕊️, but here's the thing: the work didn't die with him. The Gord Downie & Chanie Wenjack Fund built by the two families together, now runs through more than 9,500 Legacy Schools across Canada 🏫🪶, where kids learn Chanie's name and Gord's two-word command: "Do something." This week, October 17 to 22, is Secret Path Week, nine years on from his passing 📅✨. So here's my ask, crew: learn one true story you were never taught. Say Chanie Wenjack's name out loud. Support the Fund, join a Walk for Wenjack, have the hard conversation at your own table 🗣️🍽️. Gord spent his last year proving that even an ending can be a beginning, the least I can do is not waste the megaphone he handed us 🎤💛.

Forward is always a choice, and that means YOU too! 💛🚀

Sources: Gord Downie & Chanie Wenjack Fund: Our Story · Secret Path: official site · Samaritan Magazine: Secret Path Project · Dalhousie University: Legacy Space launch

🛠️🌿 The Deep Dive: 5 Things Most Pro Landscapers Won't Tell You 🌿🛠️

1. Test soil temperature before you plant a single bulb. Air temperature lies to you in October; soil temperature is the truth. Push a cheap kitchen thermometer four inches down, you want the soil consistently below about 10°C but not yet frozen before tulips and daffodils go in. Plant into soil that's still warm and the bulbs sprout tops in a mild spell, which the first real freeze then burns off, wasting the bulb's whole battery. In Edmonton that sweet window is usually late October, measure it, don't guess it.

2. Flush the salt out of your beds before the freeze locks it in. Every bed and boulevard strip that caught last winter's salted splash is still carrying sodium in the top few inches of soil. One long, slow, deep soak on a warm fall day, we're talking hours, not a sprinkle, leaches that sodium down below the root zone while the ground is still open to drain. Wait until spring and you're flushing during the thaw rush, when meltwater is already fighting you. Today's 14 degrees is exactly the kind of day to run the hose.

3. Drive your snow stakes on an angle, not straight up. A vertical stake meets a plow blade head-on and loses, it snaps or rips clean out, and the driveway edge it was guarding gets eaten by February. Angle the stake about 15 degrees away from the driveway, leaning back from the direction the plow travels, and the blade rides up and over it instead of through it. Same stake, same five minutes of work, triple the survival rate over a winter.

4. Don't nitrogen-feed trees and shrubs in the fall. A fall "winterizer" shot makes sense for the lawn, grass stores it in the roots. Woody plants play by different rules: nitrogen now pushes soft, tender new growth right when the plant should be hardening off, and the first hard freeze kills that growth back and stresses the whole shrub or tree. Let your trees and shrubs shut down on their own schedule. If they're hungry, feed them in spring when new growth can actually use it.

5. Bundle fall prunings into brush piles instead of hauling them. The default move is chip it, haul it, pay the tipping fee. The smarter move, where a client has the corner for it: stack fall prunings into a loose brush pile at the back of the yard. Chickadees and juncos shelter in them all winter, the wood breaks down in place, and you just saved a dump run and the fee that came with it. Plenty of clients love the wildlife angle once you explain it, it's the rare tip that saves money, time, AND face.
