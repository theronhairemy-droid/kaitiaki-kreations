🌟 Today's Message: The Haunting of Esther Cox: Canada's Great Amherst Mystery

In late August 1878, in a crowded little cottage on Princess Street in Amherst, Nova Scotia 🇨🇦, an 18 year old girl named Esther Cox came home from a carriage ride she would spend the rest of her life trying to forget 🏚️. A shoemaker named Bob MacNeal had tried to assault her at gunpoint out by the Tantramar Marsh, and she had survived it, shaken to her bones 😔. Days later, the knocking started. Esther and her sister Jennie heard rapping in the walls at night, then Esther began suffering fits in which her body visibly swelled and fevered, in a house she shared with her sister Olive Teed, Olive's husband Daniel, their two small children and more siblings besides 👻. Whatever had followed her home from that marsh, it was just getting started.

It escalated the way these stories are never supposed to outside of fiction 🔥. Objects flew across rooms in broad daylight. Small fires broke out on their own. Esther was slapped and pinched by hands nobody could see, and when the family doctor came, he watched her bedclothes move by themselves and heard scratching sounds he could not explain 🩺😨. Then came the detail that has kept this story alive for nearly 150 years: words appeared scratched into the wall above her bed, reading "Esther Cox, you are mine to kill." Hundreds of people came through that little house over fifteen months: clergy, journalists, physicians, the curious and the terrified, and the phenomena kept witnesses busy enough that nobody could agree on anything except that something was very wrong 📜. And when Esther fell properly ill with diphtheria, everything stopped, silent as a held breath, for the entire time she lay bedridden. The day she recovered, it started again 🕯️.

Enter Walter Hubbell, an American actor who arrived in Amherst intending to debunk the whole thing and make his name doing it 🎭. He moved into the cottage for six weeks with a notebook. He left having written the most famous ghost story Canada has ever produced: The Great Amherst Mystery, published in 1879, built from his diaries and sworn statements, and read across North America 📖🌎. But here is the part of the haunting that was never supernatural at all 💔. Esther's torment made her a spectacle while she was alive: crowds treated her house like a theatre, skeptics treated her like a fraud, and when a barn later burned, Esther herself was blamed, convicted of arson, and sent to jail for it, even though she swore to her last day the spirit she called "Bob" had set the fire 🔥. She died in 1912, having carried a haunting and a reputation in equal measure, and never once got to just be a girl something bad happened to.

Amherst, to its credit, never let her go 🕯️❤️. Her story has been retold in books and on stages for a century and a half, including a play, Guilty!, built entirely around her case, and the town now celebrates its haunted daughter every fall at Esther Fest, leaning into the mystery the way Salem leans into its witches 🎃. But the real lesson of Esther Cox is not the knocking on the walls. It is that a traumatized teenager told the adults around her that something was terribly wrong, and the world mostly answered with curiosity instead of care. So here is the call, on Halloween week of all weeks 📣. When somebody tells you their house, their life, their head is haunted, believe them first and investigate second, and be the porch light that stays on for them. And this Saturday, when the little monsters come to your door, tell them a real Canadian ghost story, the one about the girl from Amherst whose name the thing on the wall could not take from her 👻. Forward is always a choice, and that means YOU too!

🛠️🌿 The Deep Dive: 5 Things Most Pro Landscapers Won't Tell You 🌿🛠️

1. Carve the jack-o'-lantern from the bottom, not the top 🎃
Everybody saws a lid around the stem, and by Thursday the lid has shrivelled, shrunk and dropped into the candle. Cut the hole in the bottom instead: the pumpkin sits over its candle or LED puck like a lampshade, the light source stays planted on the porch instead of inside a drying gourd, and the whole lantern lifts off for relighting without a singed sleeve. Two refinements while you are in there: scrape the walls to about 2 cm thick on the face side so the features glow instead of smouldering, and rub the cut edges with a little petroleum jelly to slow the freeze-thaw shrivel that Edmonton weather guarantees by the weekend.

2. Salt, sweep and light the walkway before Saturday 🧂
Edmonton Halloween runs on a simple rule: the kids are coming whether the steps are icy or not, and half of them will be wearing costumes that restrict vision, movement and dignity in equal measure. Wednesday's snow and the freeze behind it mean Saturday's walks and porch steps can carry a skin of ice by trick-or-treat hour. Sweep the walks Friday, put a light layer of de-icer on steps and any sloped concrete before the evening rush, and make sure the path from sidewalk to door is lit the whole way. A bag of salt and ten minutes is the difference between your house being the fun stop and the incident report.

3. No real flames in pumpkins by the door 🔥🚫
Put the three ingredients together: an open candle flame, piles of dry October leaves banked against the porch, and a parade of children in flowing polyester capes brushing past within inches. That is not ambience, that is a near-miss with a date on it. Battery tealights and LED pucks have gotten bright, cheap and honestly better looking than the real thing, with none of the fire risk and no relighting trips in the cold. Save the real candles for the dinner table indoors, where the only thing they can set alight is the mood.

4. Retire the jack to the compost, not the lawn ♻️
On November 1st the jack-o'-lantern's watch ends, and where it rots matters. Left sitting on the lawn, a collapsing pumpkin smothers the turf underneath into a yellow dead patch, feeds a mould colony, and draws every wasp and neighbourhood dog in a three-block radius. Left on the walkway or drive, its slime is a genuine slip hazard on a frosty morning. Chop it into the compost or dig it into a garden bed instead: pumpkin is fast, friendly organic matter, seeds and all, and the soil will take the whole thing apart before the snow settles in for real.

5. Photograph your yard at trick-or-treat hour 📸
Saturday evening is the one time all fall your yard gets audited by a hundred small inspectors in the dark, and their stumbles are free consulting. Once the rush is on, walk your own route with a phone camera: you will see the step edge that disappears in shadow, the paver that sits a centimetre proud, the downspout drip already glazing one corner of the walk, and the stretch of path your porch light never reaches. Fix list for November, written by Halloween itself. The best landscape lighting plan in Edmonton is the one drawn from the trick-or-treater's eye level, which is roughly knee height and moving fast.

🎶 Ron Hairemy's Songs That Guarantee LAWNgevity

Canadian Track'O'The Day: "The Wreck of the Edmund Fitzgerald" by Gordon Lightfoot (clean ✅). Gordon Lightfoot of Orillia, Ontario, and this is the finest ghost story Canada ever put on the radio 🇨🇦🎶. The Edmund Fitzgerald went down on Lake Superior on November 10, 1975, with all 29 men aboard, and a year later Lightfoot turned the wreck into a six minute ballad that has never left the airwaves since. The Mariners' Church in Detroit still tolls its bell 29 times for those men every year 🔔. For me, this is Halloween in its truest Canadian form: no costume, just the lake, the gale, and the names. Play it as the light fails and Wednesday's snow starts, and listen to the whole thing 🌊.

Today's Musical Funny-Bone: "Monster Mash" by Bobby "Boris" Pickett (clean ✅). 1962, and a young Bobby Pickett doing his Boris Karloff impression so well that it became the Halloween national anthem of two countries 🧟🎤. It hit number 1, came back and hit the charts again in 1973, and the BBC banned it in '62 for being too morbid, which remains the greatest review a Halloween song has ever received 📻. For me it is mandatory listening while the candy bowl gets filled, by law, section one, paragraph graveyard smash. Play it and do the voice. You know the voice 🎃.

Hair Whippin', Head Bangin', Finger Blazin' Therapy: "Black Sabbath" by Black Sabbath (clean ✅). Birmingham, 1970, the very first track on the very first Black Sabbath album, and heavy metal being scary on purpose starts right here 🌧️🔔. Rain, a church bell, that tritone riff, and Ozzy asking what that figure standing in front of him is: fifty plus years later it still raises the hair on your arms. For me this is the heaviest song on today's list by weight of shadow alone. Play it after dark, moving the last boxes, and let the new place meet the neighbours properly 🦇.

Tongue Trippin', Bar Spittin', Beat Killin' Bars: "Mind Playing Tricks on Me" by Geto Boys (explicit 🔞, paranoia in its purest recorded form). Houston's Geto Boys, 1991, built on an Isaac Hayes sample, and Scarface delivering verses about a mind coming apart at the seams that still sound uncomfortably honest 🎤🌑. Bushwick Bill, Willie D and Face turned Halloween energy into a top 25 hit with no hook you would call friendly, and it remains the definitive rap record about being haunted by your own head. For me this is the slot used for the real darkness, the kind that does not need a costume. Play it once, properly, windows up 🚗.

Māori Track / Aotearoa Pick: "Vampire Again" by Marlon Williams (clean ✅). Marlon Williams of Lyttelton, Kai Tahu and Ngai Tai, the gothic crooner with the voice from another century 🇳🇿🧛. "Vampire Again", from 2018's Make Way for Love, is a hip-swivelling vampire love song delivered with a grin, proof that the spookiest thing in any room is usually charm. For me this one goes on while the candy gets handed out at the new place on Saturday: first Halloween, new porch, vampire on the speakers 🌙. Play it and practise your Transylvanian Christchurch accent. Nobody can stop you.

Ron Hairemy Spotlight: "Ghost of Perdition" by Opeth (clean ✅). Opeth of Stockholm, Sweden, Mikael Åkerfeldt at the helm, and this is the opening track of 2005's Ghost Reveries, ten and a half minutes that begin with one of the most famous riffs in modern metal and then wander from murder ballad quiet to full death metal storm 🌑🎸. It is a ghost story where the haunting is guilt itself: a man, a mother, and a voice that will not leave the house of his head. Sweden bookends today's whole list, Ghost B.C. at sunrise and Opeth at midnight 🇸🇪. For me this is THE Halloween closer, the one that plays after the last box is in and the porch light goes off. Play it loud enough that the new place learns your name properly 🏚️.

🌅 Conclusion

Esther Cox told the truth about a haunted house for fifteen months and the world made a spectacle of her, and nearly 150 years later her town throws her a festival, which tells you the truth does eventually get its porch light turned on 🕯️. The yard version this week is smaller and it rhymes: carve from the bottom, light with LEDs, salt the steps before the little inspectors arrive, and compost the jack when its watch ends 🎃. Shoutout to Kyle and Matt and the two trucks that made today a lift instead of a haunting, to everybody moving house in spooky season, and to every porch in Edmonton leaving the light on Saturday 📣. First Halloween in the new home, crew. So until tomorrow, this is Ron Hairemy signing off saying: Keep your stick on the ice... and may the Force be with you. Always!
