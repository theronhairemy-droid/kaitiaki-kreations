🌟 Today's Message: The Prince of the Brigade: Tommy Prince and the Fight He Kept Fighting

Some soldiers fight a war, and then some soldiers come home and have to fight their own country for the respect they already earned, and Sergeant Tommy Prince did both with the same quiet stubbornness 🎖️🌺. Thomas George Prince was born on October 25, 1915, at Petersfield, Manitoba, one of eleven children in the Brokenhead Ojibway Nation, a descendant of Chief Peguis, who led his people to the Red River in the 1790s 🌾. He grew up hunting, trapping, and tracking in the Manitoba bush, skills the army would one day call elite reconnaissance, and when Canada went to war he volunteered in June 1940, starting as a sapper with the Royal Canadian Engineers 💪. The army nearly overlooked him more than once, because Indigenous volunteers faced doors that stayed shut for reasons that had nothing to do with ability 🚪. Prince kept walking through them anyway.

By 1942 he had volunteered again, this time for the 1st Canadian Parachute Battalion, and from there into the Canadian-American First Special Service Force, the unit German soldiers named the Devil's Brigade 😈🔥. On the night of February 8, 1944, near Littoria, Italy, Prince ran some 1,400 metres of telephone wire from his lines to an abandoned farmhouse only about 200 metres from the German positions, and reported their artillery locations until the wire was cut by shellfire 📞💥. What he did next is the stuff of legend: he put on civilian clothes, grabbed a hoe, and pretended to be a local farmer weeding a field, shuffling along the line until he found the break, then bent down as if tying a shoelace and spliced the wire back together, shaking his fist at both armies for effect 🎭✨. Four German artillery positions were destroyed off his reporting, and the stunt earned him the Military Medal. In France that September he led a reconnaissance patrol deep behind enemy lines and earned the American Silver Star, making him one of only three Canadians in the whole war to hold both decorations, presented in the orbit of Buckingham Palace by King George VI himself 👑🏅.

Most men would have called that a life's worth of service, but Prince re-enlisted for Korea in 1950 with the Princess Patricia's Canadian Light Infantry and fought in the defence of Hill 677 at Kap'yong in 1951, a stand so fierce his unit received a United States Distinguished Unit Citation, the only Canadian unit ever so honoured 🪖🌏. Then he came home, and here is the part of the story Canada should sit with: the hero of two wars returned to a country where Indigenous veterans were routinely denied the land grants, loans, and benefits other veterans received, and where he could not even vote in federal elections without giving up his status 📜💔. Prince did not go quiet. He became vice-president and spokesperson for the Manitoba Indian Association and stood before a joint committee of Parliament in the 1940s arguing for education, economic opportunity, and basic fairness for his people 🗣️✊. The fight at home was slower than any patrol in Italy, and he fought it anyway, because that is who he was.

Tommy Prince died in Winnipeg on November 25, 1977, in hard circumstances, and for years his medals were scattered and lost, a national embarrassment in miniature 😔. His family and supporters tracked them down, ten of his eleven medals were recovered and are now held in trust at the Manitoba Museum, a Winnipeg street carries his name, and in 2019 he was designated a National Historic Person 🏛️🌺. This Sunday, November 8, is Indigenous Veterans Day in Canada, and Tuesday is Remembrance Day, so here is my call to action, crew: when you pin on your poppy this week, remember that some of the bravest people who ever wore this country's uniform had to fight for that country twice, once overseas and once at home, and then wear one for them on purpose 🎖️❤️. Learn his name, teach it to somebody younger, and if you pass a veteran who the history books skipped, stop and say thank you out loud. Forward is always a choice, and that means YOU too!

🛠️🌿 The Deep Dive: 5 Things Most Pro Landscapers Won't Tell You 🌿🛠️

1. Rip out the failed landscape fabric hiding under your mulch and rock beds. Weed fabric under organic mulch has a built-in expiry date: the mulch on top composts into soil, weed seeds land in that soil and root happily on top of the fabric, while the fabric itself clogs with fines until water sheets sideways off the bed instead of soaking in. By year three you have weeds, drought, and a plastic layer strangling every root that tries to cross it. On a dry week like this one, peel it out in sections while the ground is workable, and replace the system, not the sheet: cardboard under fresh mulch, or simply a proper 7 to 10 cm mulch depth topped up yearly.

2. This warm, dry week is the last honest window for exterior wood stain. Most exterior stains and deck oils need the wood dry, the air above roughly 10°C, and no rain or hard frost for 24 to 48 hours after application, a combination Edmonton almost never offers after mid-November. With highs of 9 to 14°C and no rain in the forecast through Monday, you have a real window, but work it smart: stain at midday on sun-facing boards, test moisture first because shaded wood can still be damp at noon, and skip anything that will sit below the product minimum overnight. A small section done properly this week beats a whole deck done hopefully in the cold and peeling by July.

3. Never divide ornamental grasses in the fall. Peonies love a fall division, but ornamental grasses play by opposite rules: many have hollow or soft crowns that collect meltwater, and a freshly cut, disturbed crown heading into freeze-thaw rots or heaves instead of resting. Cutting them down now also removes the winter insulation and structure that protects the crown, plus the seed heads the birds are actively eating. Tie a bright marker ribbon on the clumps you want to split, leave them standing all winter, and divide in spring the moment new green shows at the base, when the plant has energy to heal.

4. Net the cedars and yews now, before deer switch to evergreen season. Summer deer browse the garden menu, but once snow covers the ground forage, cedars, yews, and euonymus become the winter buffet, and a single hungry week can strip a hedge to bare sticks on the deer side. The fix is unglamorous and it works: drape loose black deer netting over the plant and peg it at the ground, close enough that muzzles cannot push through, loose enough that it does not crush the shape or hold wet snow against the foliage. Install it on a calm, dry day like today, check it after the first big snowfall, and remove it in spring before new growth pushes through the mesh.

5. Vacuum the leaves out of gravel beds instead of raking them. Raking a rock or gravel bed is self-defeating: the tines drag stone into the lawn, mix soil and leaf litter down into the gravel where it becomes a weed nursery, and still leave half the leaves behind. A leaf vacuum, or a blower worked low and slow in short passes on a dry day, lifts the leaf layer off while the stone stays put, and dry leaves like this week's come up clean instead of smearing into a wet mat. Work from the house outward so you are not blowing debris back onto finished sections, and empty the shredded catch straight into the compost or leaf mould pile. Ten quiet minutes per bed now saves a spring weekend of picking decomposed leaf paste out of decorative stone by hand.

Canadian Track'O'The Day: "Wake Up" by Arcade Fire (clean ✅). Arcade Fire come from Montreal, Quebec, and "Wake Up" is the surging closing anthem of their 2004 debut album Funeral, a record made in the shadow of actual family loss that somehow sounds like a stadium learning to breathe again 🇨🇦🔥. The band went on to win the Grammy for Album of the Year with The Suburbs, but this is the song that started the fire: wordless choir chorus, pounding heart, zero cynicism. For me, it is the sound a cold Edmonton morning needs, a reminder that waking up is itself an act of defiance when the year has been heavy. Play it loud on the drive and arrive already awake 🎶.

Today's Musical Funny-Bone: "The Ballad Of Bilbo Baggins" by Leonard Nimoy (clean ✅). Yes, crew, Mr. Spock himself recorded a swinging little Hobbit anthem in 1968 for his album The Two Sides of Leonard Nimoy, complete with dancing Hobbit footage that became a beloved camp artifact decades before the internet got hold of it 🖖🧙. It is earnest, it is ridiculous, and it is completely committed, which is exactly why it works: Nimoy never winks, and the song struts like Middle Earth had a house band. I love it as proof that a legend can have a sense of humour about himself and lose nothing. Queue it when the day needs its seriousness deflated 🎸.

Hair Whippin', Head Bangin', Finger Blazin' Therapy: "The Immortal" by Arch Enemy (clean ✅, full melodic death metal with harsh vocals). Arch Enemy, built in Sweden around guitarist Michael Amott after his Carcass years, open their 1999 album Burning Bridges with this one, and it is Johan Liiva on vocals, raw and furious over riffs that swing as hard as they shred 🌑🔥. Burning Bridges is the album where the band's melodic twin-guitar identity locks into place, the blueprint everything after it was built on. For me, it is perfect November fuel: dark mornings, warm cab, and a riff that refuses to let the day start slow. Play it like the frost owes you money 🎃.

Tongue Trippin', Bar Spittin', Beat Killin' Bars: "The Warriors" by Snotty Nose Rez Kids [E: explicit language]. Snotty Nose Rez Kids are Darren "Young D" Metz and Quinton "Yung Trybez" Nyce, a Haisla Nation duo from Kitamaat Village, British Columbia, who built a national name on albums like Trapline and a live show that turns every room into a community event 🎤🔥. "The Warriors" is them at full charge: proud, funny, and confrontational in the best way, carrying Indigenous resilience over a beat that struts. Dropping it the same week as Indigenous Veterans Day feels right to me, because it is the same story Tommy Prince lived, told in a new generation's language: we are still here, and we are not quiet about it. Play it with the volume up and the respect on ✊.

Māori Track / Aotearoa Pick: "Love Your Ways" by Salmonella Dub (clean ✅). Salmonella Dub formed in Christchurch in 1993 and basically invented Aotearoa's homegrown dub, reggae, and drum and bass fusion, taking it from South Island dancefloors to festival main stages 🌊🎤. "Love Your Ways," from their 2003 album One Drop East era, is their warmest calling card, and it comes from the years when their frontman was Tiki Taane, who is Māori of Ngāti Maniapoto descent, so I am filing this one as an Aotearoa pick with a genuine Māori voice at its centre, noted straight. For me, it is pure summer gravity, the sound of a country heading into long evenings while we count down to sunset at 5:55 PM. Steal a little of it for your afternoon 🌅.

Ron Hairemy Spotlight: "Semi-Charmed Life" by Third Eye Blind (clean per Spotify ✅, but be straight with yourself: the lyric is a smiling pop song about a crystal meth spiral, and that contrast is the whole point). Third Eye Blind come from San Francisco, and this was the breakout single from their 1997 self-titled debut, a top five Billboard hit with one of the most singable choruses of the decade wrapped around Stephan Jenkins' lyric about addiction and wanting something else 🎸✨. That sugar-and-rot combination is why it has lasted: it sounds like the best day of summer and reads like a warning label. For me, it is a reminder that the catchiest surfaces can hide the heaviest stories, in songs and in people, and to listen twice before judging either. Play it on the sunny drive home and actually hear it this time 🚗.
