🌟 Today's Message: The Edmonton Kid Who Moved Time: Michael J. Fox

Michael J. Fox was born on June 9, 1961, right here in Edmonton 🇨🇦, a military kid who bounced from base to base before the family settled in Burnaby, BC 🌲. He dropped out of school to act, landed Family Ties as Alex P. Keaton, and then in 1985 Back to the Future turned a 24-year-old Canadian into the biggest movie star on the planet 🎬⚡. For a solid decade, the man simply could not miss, the charm, the timing, the face on every lunchbox in North America 🌎. If you wanted a picture of a life with no visible ceiling, his was it ✨.

Then, in 1991, filming in Florida, he noticed his pinky finger twitching and it wouldn't stop 😔. The diagnosis: young-onset Parkinson's disease, at thirty years old, at the peak of everything. He hid it for seven years 💔, working constantly, drinking too much, terrified that the day the story broke, the career and the identity went with it. Imagine carrying that secret through every interview, every set, every premiere, performing lightness for a living while your own body quietly rebelled against you 🎭. In 1998 he finally told the world, on his own terms, and what he did next is the reason this story belongs in this blog 🔥.

He decided to act, and not small 🎯. In 2000 he stepped back from Spin City and founded The Michael J. Fox Foundation with a goal most charities are too polite to say out loud: find the cure, then put itself out of business 🧪. Not galas for their own sake, research money, aimed like a tool, with deadlines attached. He became the most famous patient-advocate on Earth, testified before Congress, wrote bestselling memoirs that treated his disease with more honesty and humour than anyone expected 📚, and kept acting whenever his body allowed, because the work was never the problem. Quitting was 💪.

The lasting impact has a number on it: his foundation has funded over two billion dollars of Parkinson's research, the largest nonprofit funder of it in the world 💰🔬, and in 2023 the science it backed landed a breakthrough the field had chased for decades: a biomarker test that can detect Parkinson's pathology in spinal fluid with about 90% accuracy, even before symptoms 🧬🎉. Canada made him an Officer of the Order of Canada in 2010 and has since promoted him to Companion, its highest rank 🎖️. Yesterday's blog honoured Tommy Douglas on his birthday, a man who turned one act of free care into medicare. Today's honours an Edmonton-born kid who turned a devastating diagnosis into two billion dollars of hope for strangers 🌍. Different fights, same refusal. So here's my call to action: somebody in your orbit is carrying a diagnosis quietly right now, be the person who asks how they are, and then keeps asking 📣. Forward is always a choice, and that means YOU too!

🛠️🌿 The Deep Dive: 5 Things Most Pro Landscapers Won't Tell You 🌿🛠️

1. Swap the sump-pump discharge to rigid pipe before Friday 🚰
That flexible summer discharge hose is a freeze trap: it sags, and every low spot holds water. Friday brings a 90% rain day followed by a hard freeze, pooled water in the hose freezes solid, and the next melt the pump pushes against a plug of ice and backs up into the basement instead 💧❄️. Swap now to rigid, larger-diameter pipe with a continuous downward slope away from the house and no dips anywhere in the run. Ten metres of straight, sloped pipe in October beats one flooded basement in February, every single time 🏠.

2. Never plow snow off a septic field ❄️🚫
It feels backwards, but the snow ON a septic field is insulation, not obstruction. Plow it off (or drive and park on the field all winter) and you strip the blanket that keeps frost shallow, the cold drives deeper, lines and the field itself can freeze, and vehicle weight compacts frozen soil right where you least want compaction 🚗. Leave the field's snow exactly where it falls, keep every tire off it, and let the blanket do the engineering. The ugliest snow on the property is doing the most work 💪.

3. Don't spread fireplace wood ash on Edmonton beds 🔥
The old-country advice says wood ash feeds the garden, and on acidic soil, it can. Edmonton clay-loam is not acidic: it typically runs alkaline already, around pH 7.4–8+ 🧪. Ash is both alkaline and salty, so spreading it pushes pH higher still and starts locking up iron and manganese, that's how you get yellow, chlorotic plants in soil that's technically full of nutrients. Unless a soil test actually shows acidity, your ash belongs in the garbage or the compost's story ends badly. Test first; amend second 📋.

4. Guard shrubs under metal-roof eaves from roof-avalanche snow 🏠❄️
A metal roof doesn't shed snow in polite little handfuls, it lets go in one sliding sheet, all at once, straight down the eave line. Any shrub planted under that line takes the whole roof's snowpack on the crown in a single second: leaders snap, crowns split, and the plant you find in spring looks like it lost an argument with a truck 🌿💥. Mark the slide zones now, while you can still see the eaves clearly, protect keepers with a sturdy wooden A-frame, and plan to move anything precious out of the fall line entirely.

5. Rodent-proof winter storage 🐭
A mower deck put away dirty is a mouse hotel with the heat already on: packed grass and soil make perfect nesting material, and a warm shed does the rest, by spring you're looking at chewed wiring and a nest where the air filter used to be 🔧. Scrape and wash the deck before it goes in. Same logic for grass seed: an open paper bag in the garage is an all-winter buffet. Move seed into sealed metal bins with tight lids, and the mice can file their complaints elsewhere 🗄️.
