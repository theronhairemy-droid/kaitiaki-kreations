🌟 Today's Message: The Runner Who Ran Home: Tom Longboat

Tom Longboat was born on July 4, 1886, on the Six Nations of the Grand River reserve in Ontario 🇨🇦, an Onondaga kid whose name, Cogwagee, means "Everything" 🌿. Like so many Indigenous kids of his era, he was sent away to the Mohawk Institute Residential School in Brantford 🏫, a place built to take his language and his identity, and Tom made his decision the only way he knew how: he ran away. Twice. The second time he ran all the way home and stayed there, working odd jobs with his uncle, running everywhere, to the fields, after the cows, down roads nobody else bothered timing him on 🏃💨. That run home was not an escape from his story. It was the start of it ✨.

Because the kid who ran home turned out to be able to outrun just about everybody on the continent 🔥. In 1906 he won Hamilton's Around the Bay race, the oldest road race in North America, by more than three minutes while spectators laughed at the idea of betting on him 😤. Then on April 19, 1907, in cold, rain, and blowing sleet, he lined up for the Boston Marathon and won it in 2:24:24, nearly five minutes faster than the previous record, at one point racing a train that threatened to cut across his route 🚂💥. He represented Canada at the 1908 London Olympics, collapsed in the brutal heat like half the field, then came back in 1909 to win the world professional marathon championship at Madison Square Garden in New York 🏆. A boy the system tried to erase had become one of the first Canadian athletes to make a living from sport alone, and one of the most famous runners alive 🌍.

And still the world found ways to make it harder for him 💔. The sports press mocked his training, because Tom alternated savage hard days with long easy walks, and writers of the day sneered at it as laziness instead of seeing what every modern coach now knows: he had figured out hard and easy day periodized training decades before it had a name 🧠⚡. He was falsely accused, doubted, and written off, and he answered the way he always had, by winning more races and breaking his own bests until the criticism ran out of breath before he did 🥇. Then, at 29, at the height of his fame, he set the career aside and enlisted for the First World War 🎖️. The fastest man in Canada became a dispatch runner with the 107th Pioneer Battalion in France, literally running messages between units through shellfire, getting wounded, getting reported dead at one point, and still winning the eight mile race at the 1918 Canadian Corps Dominion Day sports day 🏃🔥. Even in a war, the man ran.

Tom Longboat died in 1949, back home on the reserve where it all started 🕯️, and Canada has been trying to say his name properly ever since. He is in Canada's Sports Hall of Fame, he was named a National Historic Person in 1976, and Maclean's voted him the number one sports figure of the 20th century 🇨🇦🏅. Since 1951 the Tom Longboat Awards have honoured the top Indigenous athletes in this country every year, which means every young Indigenous runner lacing up today is quite literally running in a lane he cut 🌟. His lesson is not complicated and it is not soft: when the place you are in is trying to make you smaller, running toward home, toward your people, toward who you actually are, is not quitting. It is the whole race. So check on the runner in your own life today, the one doing the hard miles with no crowd, and be the voice in their ear that says keep going 📣❤️. Forward is always a choice, and that means YOU too!

🛠️🌿 The Deep Dive: 5 Things Most Pro Landscapers Won't Tell You 🌿🛠️

1. Use today's rain as a free eavestrough leak test: chalk the drips now, seal them on the next dry window 🌧️
Heavy rain is the only honest eavestrough inspection there is, and today Edmonton is getting about 5 mm of it with the worst falling this morning. Walk the house while it pours and watch the joints, corners, and downspout seams: drips, waterfalls over the edge, and stains down the siding mark exactly where the system fails, and a stick of chalk or a phone photo pins the spot before you forget it. Do not try to seal it wet: most sealants need a dry surface and a few degrees above freezing to cure, which makes Monday's dry 5°C the fix day and today purely the diagnosis. A leaking joint you ignore in October becomes an ice dam's water supply in January, dripping meltwater straight at the foundation it was built to protect 🧊.

2. Close foundation and crawlspace vents before tonight's freeze gets a vote on your pipes 🚪
Open foundation vents made sense in July, when they aired out the crawlspace. In late October they are just a funnel delivering -6°C Saturday night air directly onto the plumbing that runs under the floor. Close the vents, or block them with fitted foam board from inside, before the first hard freeze weekend, and leave them closed until spring thaw is genuinely done, not the first warm afternoon. The pipes most likely to burst in an Edmonton house are rarely the ones in the warm centre of the basement; they are the ones in an exterior wall or a vented crawlspace that nobody thought about until the water was already coming through the ceiling. Ten minutes with a flashlight today beats a plumber's emergency rate in February 🔦.

3. Roof heat cables go on BEFORE the first ice: zigzagged above the eave line, never stapled, never just lying in the gutter 🔌
Every fall somebody climbs a ladder in December to install heat cable onto a roof that is already an ice rink, which is exactly backwards and how people get hurt. The cable belongs in a zigzag clipped along the eave, reaching up past the wall line where ice dams actually form, with a loop down through the downspout, because a cable lying flat in the gutter melts a tunnel through the ice and changes nothing. Never drive staples or nails through the cable, that punctures the insulation and creates the short you were trying to avoid, and plug it into a ground fault protected outlet you have actually tested. On a north facing Edmonton roof with any ice dam history, installing this weekend, on a dry shingle, in daylight, is the whole trick ❄️.

4. Stockpile traction sand and grit DRY and indoors today, before tonight's rain and freeze weld the outdoor pile into one lump 🪣
Here is the trap built into today's exact forecast: it rains all morning, then it freezes tonight and stays below zero all weekend. Any sand, grit, or gravel sitting in an open outdoor pile or an open bin soaks up that rain and wakes up Saturday as a single frozen brick you have to attack with a pick before you can sand a single step. So move the winter traction supply under cover today, into lidded bins or buckets in the garage or by the door, with a scoop living in each one. Dry sand spreads, flows, and actually lands where the ice is; a frozen lump gets carried, dropped, and abandoned by the third morning. Come the first black ice dawn, the crew with dry grit is working while everybody else is still chiselling 🧊.

5. Photograph your irrigation controller wiring and zone map before you kill the power for winter 📸
The blowout gets all the glory, but the spring startup horror story is almost always electrical: a controller unplugged for six months, a common wire that came loose, three zones nobody can match to the yard anymore. Before you power the system down, open the controller and photograph the wiring exactly as it sits, every coloured wire on its terminal, then screenshot or sketch the zone map with what each zone actually waters. Tape a printed copy inside the controller door. In April, when a zone will not fire and the client is watching, that photo turns a two hour diagnostic hunt into a five minute fix, and it costs you ninety seconds today. Pros are not smarter in spring; they just left themselves better notes in fall 🗒️.

🎶 Ron Hairemy's Songs That Guarantee LAWNgevity

Canadian Track'O'The Day: "Shook" by Shawn Desman (clean ✅). Toronto's Shawn Desman, born Shawn Fernandes, and "Shook" was the debut single off his self titled 2002 debut album, the song that put a Canadian R&B pop kid on every radio in the country overnight 🇨🇦🎤. Desman went on to a long career as a singer, dancer, and choreographer, and the track still sounds like pure early 2000s Canadian pop confidence. For me it is a move weekend song: everything rattled, everything boxed, and the groove still holds 📦. Play it while the last room gets packed and try not to dance with a box in your hands. You will fail 💃.

Today's Musical Funny-Bone: "Rabbit" by Chas & Dave (clean ✅). Chas Hodges and Dave Peacock, London's finest cockney piano and bass duo, and "Rabbit" was their 1980 signature hit, built on the old slang that to rabbit means to talk and talk and then talk some more 🐰🎹. The whole joke is one affectionate groan: he loves her, she is wonderful, she just never, ever stops chatting. For me this one is pure pub warmth, the sound of two mates who played a thousand working men's clubs and earned every laugh the honest way 😄. Play it on the drive, sing the chorus at full volume, and check whether anybody in the truck has gone quiet. They have not 🎶.

Hair Whippin', Head Bangin', Finger Blazin' Therapy: "Ravenous" by Arch Enemy (clean ✅). Swedish melodic death metal, Arch Enemy, built by guitarist Michael Amott out of the ashes of Carcass, and "Ravenous" is the opener's punch off 2001's Wages of Sin, the first album with Angela Gossow on vocals 🇸🇪🔥. That record is where the band's modern sound locks in: twin guitar harmonies flying over a rhythm section with bad intentions. For me this is the Arch Enemy run continuing by popular demand, and a Friday storm cannot compete with this riff for sheer forward motion 💪. Play it while the rain hammers the truck roof and feel extremely prepared for winter 🌧️.

Tongue Trippin', Bar Spittin', Beat Killin' Bars: "Powerless" by Classified (clean ✅, but heavy subject matter, handled straight). Classified is Luke Boyd from Enfield, Nova Scotia, a Juno winning rapper and producer who built a national career without ever leaving the East Coast 🇨🇦🎤. He released "Powerless" in April 2018 and called it some of the most important words he has ever written: the song and its video, filmed on Millbrook First Nation in Truro with Mi'kmaq drummers and dancers, take on child maltreatment, violence against women and Indigenous people, and the national inquiry into Missing and Murdered Indigenous Women and Girls. For me this is the slot used properly: bars carrying real weight, from stories real people trusted him with 🕯️. Play it once, properly, not as background, and sit with what it asks of you.

Māori Track / Aotearoa Pick: "For the People" by Nesian Mystik (clean ✅). Auckland's Nesian Mystik formed in a music room at Western Springs College, a crew bringing Māori, Tongan, Samoan, and Cook Island ancestry into one sound, with main songwriter Te Awanui "Awa" Reeder carrying the Māori line in the group 🇳🇿🌿. "For the People" was a single off their 2002 debut Polysaturated, an album that debuted at number 1 in Aotearoa and went four times platinum, and the single itself went Gold and Top 5. For me the title alone earns its place on a Friday when everything I am doing is for my people and my next chapter 🌱. Play it with the windows down on a spring Auckland afternoon, or in a rainy Edmonton truck pretending, both work 🌏.

Ron Hairemy Spotlight: "Shine" by Collective Soul (clean ✅). Collective Soul out of Stockbridge, Georgia, and "Shine" is the song that dragged them out of demo land in 1993 and onto every rock station on the continent when it was re released on 1994's Hints, Allegations, and Things Left Unsaid ✨🎸. Singer Ed Roland wrote it as a kid in his parents' house, and it became the band's breakout, the "yeah" heard around the world. This one comes straight off my own "All time faves in the spotlight!" shelf, and for me it is the light at the end of a packing tunnel: one more box, one more trip, and then a new place with my name moving toward its new name on the door 🔑. Play it on the last load, loud 🚚.

That's the goal. That's LAWNgevity. 🌱🔥

🌅 Conclusion

Tom Longboat ran home from a school built to erase him and then outran the whole continent, which tells you everything about what a person can do once they are running toward their own people instead of away from somebody else's idea of them 🏃🇨🇦. The yard version today is smaller and it rhymes: watch the rain like a pro, chalk the leaks, close the vents, and get the grit under cover before tonight's freeze locks every mistake in ice 🧊. Shoutout to every Indigenous athlete carrying the Longboat name forward, to Kyle and the chain gang getting these boxes staged, and to everybody with a battle, a move, or a hard race on their weekend 📣. Run your race, crew. So until tomorrow, this is Ron Hairemy signing off saying: Keep your stick on the ice... and may the Force be with you. Always!
