🌟 Today's Message: Women Are Persons: The Famous Five

Let me tell you about five Alberta women who refused to accept the answer they'd been given 🇨🇦💪. Emily Murphy of Edmonton, the first female magistrate in the British Empire 👩‍⚖️, had her sights on the Senate, but she kept hitting the same wall: under the British North America Act, only "qualified persons" could be appointed, and the government of the day insisted women simply weren't persons under the law 🚧. Imagine being told by your own country's rulebook that you don't legally count as a person 😤. Murphy didn't sulk about it, she recruited. She brought together Henrietta Muir Edwards, Nellie McClung, Louise McKinney and Irene Parlby, writers, legislators and organizers, every one of them a force in her own right 🌟, and on August 27, 1927, the five of them petitioned Ottawa to put the question to the Supreme Court: does the word "persons" include female persons? 📜

The challenge they were up against was bigger than one Senate seat 🧱. Most Canadian women could vote by then, some could sit in provincial legislatures and even the House of Commons, but the Senate door stayed locked, and the logic locking it said half the country stood outside the legal definition of a person 🔒. In 1928, the Supreme Court of Canada answered their question with a unanimous "no" ❌. Five weeks of argument, centuries of tradition cited, and the door slammed. A lot of movements would have died right there on the courthouse steps 💔. The Famous Five treated "no" as a comma, not a period, they appealed over the Supreme Court's head to the Judicial Committee of the Privy Council in London, then the highest court of appeal in the British Empire ⚖️🌍.

That decision to act, to take the fight one court higher instead of going home, changed the country 🇨🇦✨. On October 18, 1929, ninety-seven years ago this Sunday, the Privy Council overturned the Supreme Court and ruled that women are "qualified persons," eligible for the Senate 🎉. The judgment called the exclusion of women from public office a relic of days more barbarous than ours, and its "living tree" view of the Constitution still shapes Canadian law today 🌳⚖️. Cairine Wilson became Canada's first woman senator in 1930. The bittersweet footnote: Emily Murphy herself never got the seat, she was passed over, and died in 1933 without sitting in the chamber she opened for everyone after her 🕯️. She built a door she never walked through, and she built it anyway.

The lasting impact is written into the country itself 💛. October is Women's History Month in Canada, October 18 is Persons Day, the Famous Five stand in bronze on Parliament Hill and in Calgary, and their faces have graced the fifty-dollar bill 🏛️. Every woman who has sat in the Senate since 1930 walked through the opening those five Albertans pried loose with a petition and sheer refusal to quit 🔥. So here's my call to action: this Persons Day weekend, learn one of their names properly, Murphy, McClung, McKinney, Edwards, Parlby, and pass the story on to a young person in your life 📣. Rights don't drift forward on their own; somebody has to push. Forward is always a choice, and that means YOU too!

🛠️🌿 The Deep Dive: 5 Things Most Pro Landscapers Won't Tell You 🌿🛠️

1. Lift and store your dahlia, canna and gladiolus tubers after the first frost 🌺
Dahlias, cannas and glads are not Zone 3 hardy, leave them in Edmonton ground and winter turns the tubers to mush. Wait until the first frost blackens the foliage (that hit is what signals the plant to pull its energy down), then dig carefully with a fork well outside the clump, cure them a few days somewhere dry and airy, and store them in barely-dry peat or perlite at 4–7°C. Label every variety before storage, in spring, every tuber looks identical and your memory is lying to you.

2. Build a sand-and-oil bucket to clean your hand tools 🪣
Fill a 5-gallon bucket with dry sand and stir in just enough oil (linseed or even clean vegetable oil) to make it feel like damp beach sand. Every time you finish with a shovel, spade or pruners, plunge the blade in and out a few times, the sand scours off soil and sap, and the thin oil film left behind stops rust cold. Tools stored all winter after a sand-bucket pass come out in spring clean and sharp-ish instead of orange and seized, and it takes ten seconds a tool.

3. Sow winter rye on empty vegetable beds right now 🌾
Bare garden soil in Alberta spends winter getting wind-scoured and freeze-thaw beaten, and spring finds it crusted and lifeless. Winter rye is the toughest cover crop there is, it germinates in soil as cold as about 1°C, so a mid-October sowing still takes, and it holds the bed with living roots all winter, suppresses early weeds, and gets tilled in come spring as free organic matter. Broadcast it thick, rake it in shallow, and let today's warm spell do the rest.

4. Move latex paint, caulk and liquid chemicals into heated storage before the cold nights hit 🎨
Sunday and Monday nights are touching 0°C and Tuesday drops to −1°C, and one hard freeze quietly ruins a fortune in supplies. Latex paint that freezes turns to cottage-cheese lumps and never recovers, caulking and adhesives separate or skin over in the cartridge, and liquid fertilizers and pesticides can split their containers as they expand. Ten minutes today moving it all into a heated space saves re-buying it in spring at spring prices.

5. Clear wet leaves out of window wells and away from the foundation before freeze-up 🍂
A window well full of wet leaves is a dam, not a decoration, it traps meltwater against the foundation and the basement window, and once that soggy mass freezes it expands right where you least want pressure. Same story for leaf mats piled against foundation walls and stairwells: they hold moisture against concrete through every freeze-thaw cycle of winter. Today's dry 18°C is the day to rake them out, check the well drains are clear, and send that water away from the house instead of storing it against the wall.
